Ship Routine & Leave

Port of Leith, Edinburgh

Per Routine Orders — Edinburgh. Confirm whether the ship is alongside at Leith or at anchor in the Firth of Forth (with boat transfers) — it changes your return-to-ship timings.

Per Routine Orders.

Per Routine Orders.

  • Currency: Pound sterling (£). Scottish banknotes (Bank of Scotland, Royal Bank of Scotland, Clydesdale) are legal tender in Scotland and are accepted alongside Bank of England notes, though some shops in England may refuse them. Contactless card and phone payments are accepted almost everywhere.
  • ATMs: Widely available across Leith and the city centre, including at Ocean Terminal. Withdrawals are free from most UK machines.
  • Language: English, with a strong Scots accent and plenty of dialect. Gaelic is only occasionally heard, mostly on signage.
  • Phones: Check your roaming plan before landing. UK country code is +44; Edinburgh landlines start 0131. Power sockets are Type G (three-pin, 230V) — bring an adaptor.
  • Drinking age: 18. Scotland has a minimum unit price on alcohol, so drinks are not especially cheap.
  • Have your hotel name, address, and phone number ready before requesting leave.
  • Provide a contact number where you can be reached at all times.
  • Know your leave expiry time and plan return travel accordingly.
  • If your plans change, inform the ship per routine as soon as possible.
  • Keep your military ID and leave pass on your person.
  • Edinburgh is a big, busy city. Leith is about 3–4 km from the centre — allow time for buses or trams on the way back, particularly at night.

Weather

Edinburgh & Leith
🌫️
Four seasons in one day. Edinburgh weather changes quickly, and the east coast gets haar — cold sea fog off the Forth. Always check a live source before heading out.
What to expect
Edinburgh has a mild, temperate maritime climate — cool, changeable, and often breezy. Summers (June–August) are comfortable, with highs around 18–19°C, and very long days (sunset after 10 pm at midsummer). Winters are cold and damp, with highs around 6°C, lows near 1°C and frequent night frosts, and the sun sets around 3:40 pm in mid-December. Wind off the Firth of Forth makes it feel colder than the thermometer says, and the haar can roll in on spring and summer mornings. Pack a waterproof and layers year-round.

Getting Around

The port is in Leith, about 3–4 km from the city centre. The Old Town and New Town are compact and very walkable once you get there, but hilly and cobbled. Trams and buses link Leith to Princes Street.

Transport
🚊
Tram
Runs from the Airport through Haymarket, Princes Street and down Leith Walk to Newhaven, about every 7 minutes in the daytime. The Shore, Port of Leith and Ocean Terminal stops are the closest to the ship. Check edinburghtrams.com for current fares and last trams.
🚌
Bus
Lothian Buses run frequently between Leith and the city centre, with night services too. Pay by contactless card or the Lothian app; daily caps apply. See lothianbuses.com for current fares.
🚕
Taxis & rideshare
Black cabs can be hailed or found at ranks, and phone numbers are on the Local Contacts page. Private hire cars must be pre-booked; Uber operates in the city.
🚶
Walking
Leith to the Old Town is roughly 45–60 minutes on foot along Leith Walk. Fine in good weather, but hilly at the city end.
🚆
Train
Waverley Station is in the heart of the city and has services across Scotland and to London. Haymarket handles west and Fife services.
🚗
Drive on the left
If renting a car, note that the UK drives on the left. Parking in the centre is limited and expensive; public transport is easier.
🌉
Day trips
South Queensferry (Forth Bridges) is close by train or bus. Stirling, St Andrews, and Glasgow are all within about an hour or so by train.

Food & Drink

Seafood, whisky, and a lot of pubs
🍴
Edinburgh has an excellent food scene for its size — fresh Scottish seafood, game, and a strong specialty-coffee culture, plus the Michelin-starred restaurants of Leith right by the port. Book dinner ahead, especially at weekends and during the Festival in August. Try a traditional haggis, neeps & tatties at least once.
🏨

Hotels

Book early — the city fills up
🛏️
Edinburgh has hotels at every price point, but demand is high — particularly in August (the Festival Fringe and the Military Tattoo) and at Hogmanay (New Year). Book as early as possible. Staying in Leith keeps you close to the ship; staying in the Old or New Town puts you in the middle of the sights.

Safety

Stay sharp, stay safe
🚨 UK Emergency Numbers

112Also works 101Police non-emergency 111NHS 24
  • Edinburgh is one of the safer UK cities, but it is a large, busy capital with a lively nightlife. Normal big-city awareness applies.
  • Pickpockets work crowded areas — the Royal Mile, Princes Street, and Waverley Station, especially during the Festival.
  • Stay aware of your surroundings at night, particularly around the Grassmarket, Cowgate, and Rose Street.
  • Traffic drives on the left. Look right first when crossing, and watch for trams and buses.
  • Buddy system, especially at night.
  • The weather turns quickly. Carry a waterproof and a warm layer even on a sunny morning.
  • Cobbles and steps can be very slippery when wet, especially in the closes off the Royal Mile.
  • Arthur's Seat and Salisbury Crags have steep, loose paths and exposed edges. Wear proper footwear and avoid them in low cloud, high wind, or after dark.
  • Haar (sea fog) can reduce visibility along the waterfront and on the hills.
  • Winter daylight is short — sunset is around 3:40 pm in mid-December.
  • The pubs are good and plentiful. Pace yourself — pints and whisky measures are generous.
  • Know how you're getting back to the ship before you go out. Night buses run, and taxis and Uber are available, but expect delays at closing time.
  • The council has byelaws banning drinking alcohol in designated public places. Keep it to licensed premises.
  • Look out for your shipmates.
  • You're representing the ship — be a good guest.
  • Carry your military ID and a second piece of government photo ID. Bars may ask for photo ID.
  • Don't carry more cash than you need — cards and contactless are accepted nearly everywhere.
  • Keep phones and wallets in front pockets or zipped bags in crowds.
  • Leave valuables secured aboard or in your hotel safe.
  • Call 999 (or 112) for police, fire, ambulance, or the Coastguard.
  • Call 101 for non-emergency police matters.
  • Call 111 (NHS 24) for urgent but non-life-threatening medical advice.
  • Hospital: Royal Infirmary of Edinburgh, 51 Little France Crescent — Emergency Department open 24 hours, for ages 16 and over. Children go to the Royal Hospital for Children and Young People.
  • Follow your ship's medical and insurance procedures before seeking non-emergency care ashore.
  • Inform the ship of any incidents as soon as practical.

Edinburgh & Leith History

Castle, Enlightenment, and a very old port
A Capital Built on a Volcano 🌋
Edinburgh has been Scotland's capital for over 600 years. Its Old Town sits on a ridge of volcanic rock leading down from the Castle, while the Georgian New Town to the north is a planned masterpiece of neoclassical streets and squares. Together they form a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Leith, where your ship is berthed, was Edinburgh's port for centuries and has its own long and colourful maritime story.

Edinburgh Castle stands on Castle Rock, an extinct volcanic plug, and the site has been occupied since the Iron Age. For centuries it was a royal residence and the key fortress in Scotland's wars with England.

The Royal Mile runs down the ridge from the Castle to the Palace of Holyroodhouse. Confined by the city walls, the medieval town grew upward — tenements of ten or more storeys, with narrow closes and wynds running between them. Many of those closes are still walkable today.

The Castle's One O'Clock Gun has been fired since 1861 — daily at 1 pm except Sundays, Good Friday and Christmas Day. It was added as a sound signal for ships in the Forth, because haar often hid the time ball on Calton Hill.

In the 18th century Edinburgh became one of Europe's great intellectual centres. Philosophers and scientists including David Hume, Adam Smith, and James Hutton worked here, and the period became known as the Scottish Enlightenment.

As the Old Town grew overcrowded, the city held a design competition in 1766 and began a new, planned district to the north. The New Town's wide streets, squares, and classical buildings are still among the finest examples of Georgian town planning anywhere. The Old and New Towns were inscribed as a World Heritage Site in 1995.

Leith appears in records from 1128 and became Edinburgh's official port in 1329. It was an independent burgh from 1833 until it merged with Edinburgh in 1920, against the wishes of many Leithers — some locals still insist it's a separate place.

Leith has a long link to France through the Auld Alliance, and in 1560 English and Scottish Protestant forces took the town. Leith later became a major whaling port (from the early 17th century into the 1950s), a shipbuilding centre (until 1984) and a centre for storing wine and whisky. Reminders of that history are still visible around the Shore.

The Royal Yacht Britannia, launched in 1953 and decommissioned in 1997, has been permanently moored at Ocean Terminal in Leith since 1998.

Scotch whisky is legally defined and must be distilled and aged in Scotland for at least three years. Leith was a major centre for storing wine and whisky, and the port shipped it around the world.

Whisky is made in several regions, each with its own style — smoky Islay, honeyed Speyside, light Lowland, and more. Edinburgh is a good place to try a range side by side; the Scotch Whisky Experience and the Bow Bar are two popular options. Whisky is usually served neat or with a splash of water — ask the bartender.

Every August, Edinburgh hosts the world's largest arts festival — the Fringe — alongside the International Festival, the Book Festival, and the Royal Edinburgh Military Tattoo on the Castle Esplanade. The city gets extremely busy and prices rise accordingly.

Hogmanay (New Year's Eve) is the other big event, with street parties and fireworks. Since 1999 the city has also been home to the Scottish Parliament, which sits at Holyrood.
