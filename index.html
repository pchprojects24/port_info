Ship Routine & Leave

Cancún, Quintana Roo

Per Routine Orders — Cancún. Confirm whether the ship will be alongside or at anchor with boat transfers, and where the landing point is. It changes your return-to-ship timings, and boat transfers stop when the port is closed for weather.

Per Routine Orders.

Per Routine Orders.

  • Currency: Mexican peso (MXN). US dollars are taken in tourist areas, often at a poor rate — pesos usually work out cheaper, especially for taxis and off-resort spending.
  • ATMs: Use bank-owned machines (Banorte, BBVA, Santander, Banamex) rather than airport exchange counters or standalone ATMs in tourist zones.
  • Tipping: Expected. Around 10–15% in restaurants and on tours; small amounts in pesos for other service staff.
  • Language: Spanish. English is widely spoken in the Hotel Zone; a few words of Spanish go a long way downtown.
  • Phones: Check your roaming plan before arriving. Mexico's country code is +52.
  • Power: 127 V with Type A/B plugs — the same as Canada, so no adaptor needed.
  • Time zone: UTC−5 all year (Quintana Roo has not used daylight saving time since 2015).
  • Have your hotel name, address, and phone number ready before requesting leave.
  • Provide a contact number where you can be reached at all times.
  • Know your leave expiry time and plan return travel accordingly — including boat transfers, which stop in bad weather.
  • If your plans change, inform the ship per routine as soon as possible.
  • Keep your military ID and leave pass on your person.
  • The Hotel Zone is a long, narrow strip. Allow plenty of time to get back from the far end, especially at night.

Weather

Cancún & the Hotel Zone
🌀
Hurricane season runs June to November, with the highest risk in September and October. Watch the National Hurricane Center and follow ship, hotel and civil-protection instructions if a storm is forecast.
What to expect
Cancún is hot and humid all year. The rainy season runs roughly May to October, often with short, heavy downpours followed by sunshine; November to April is the drier, more comfortable season. In October expect highs around 29–30°C and lows in the mid-20s, with the sea at its warmest. Sargassum seaweed can pile up on beaches, mainly in spring and summer — 2026 was forecast to be a record year. Pack light clothes, a hat, reef-safe sunscreen and a light rain jacket.

Getting Around

Tap a place to open it in Google Maps
📍
Cancún has two halves. The Hotel Zone is a long, narrow island strip of beaches and resorts along the Boulevard Kukulcán. Downtown (El Centro) is on the mainland, with markets, local food and lower prices. Buses run between the two all day.

Transport
🚌
City buses (R-1, R-2, R-27)
Run about every 10 minutes between downtown and the Hotel Zone, roughly 5 am to midnight. Fares are only a few pesos — carry small change, as drivers prefer exact fare.
🚕
Taxis
No meters: fares are set by zone, so agree the price before you get in. Fares often rise at night. Use official taxis from hotel ranks.
📱
Uber & DiDi
Uber has operated legally in Quintana Roo since a 2023 court ruling, but there have been tense, sometimes violent disputes with taxi drivers. Airport pickups in particular can be unreliable.
⛴️
Ferry to Isla Mujeres
Ultramar sails from Puerto Juárez (downtown, usually cheapest) and from Hotel Zone piers such as Playa Tortugas. The crossing takes about 20–30 minutes. Check the timetable, as there are fewer sailings off-season.
🚍
ADO coaches
Comfortable long-distance buses from the downtown terminal and the airport to Playa del Carmen, Tulum and beyond.
🚆
Tren Maya
The new Maya Train runs from the Cancún Aeropuerto station, including a route to Mérida via Chichén Itzá (about 3½ hours). Timetables change by season — check before you travel.
🚗
Driving
Mexico drives on the right. Watch for speed bumps (topes), and get full insurance if renting.

Food & Drink

Seafood, tacos, and a lot of nightlife
🍴
For the most authentic Yucatecan and Mexican food at the best prices, head downtown — around Parque de las Palapas, Avenida Yaxchilán and the markets. The Hotel Zone has the lagoon-view restaurants and the big nights out. Try cochinita pibil, ceviche and fish tacos. Avoid ice and salads where you're unsure of the water.
🏨

Hotels

Hotel Zone or downtown?
🛏️
The Hotel Zone is the easiest beach base. The northern stretch (about Km 4–12) has calmer water and keeps restaurants, ferries and nightlife close. Downtown is cheaper and better for local food, with buses to the beach. Many Hotel Zone resorts are all-inclusive, so check what's covered.

Safety

Sun, sea, and street smarts

  • Canada advises a high degree of caution for Mexico because of crime and kidnapping. Check travel.gc.ca for the current wording and any regional warnings.
  • United States: Quintana Roo is rated "exercise increased caution" (May 2026).
  • Most reported incidents affecting tourists happen downtown or in nightlife areas after dark.
  • Green = safe · Yellow = caution · Red = no swimming. Red flags are common, and swimmers who ignore them get into trouble — on one day, 18 people had to be rescued.
  • Open-sea beaches such as Delfines, Ballenas and Marlín have the strongest surf. Bay-side beaches (Tortugas, Langosta, Caracol) are calmer.
  • Caught in a rip current? Stay calm. It pulls you out, not under — swim parallel to the shore, then back in.
  • Sargassum seaweed can make some beaches unpleasant and change the currents. Check the local sargassum alerts.
  • Don't drink the tap water. Use bottled or filtered water, and be wary of ice unless you know it is filtered.
  • The sun is intense. Hat, shade and plenty of water — heat exhaustion comes on quickly.
  • Use reef-safe sunscreen. In protected areas such as Puerto Morelos Reef National Park, only sunscreen free of oxybenzone and octinoxate is allowed.
  • Open bars are everywhere — pace yourself. Never leave a drink unattended.
  • Pickpocketing is the main risk in busy clubs. Carry only what you need.
  • Stick to well-lit, busy streets after dark, and avoid isolated areas.
  • Never buy drugs. It's illegal, and the drug trade is behind the violence that shapes the travel advisories.
  • Know how you're getting back to the ship before you go out, and agree any taxi fare up front.
  • Buddy system — look out for your shipmates. You're representing the ship.
  • Carry your military ID and a second piece of government photo ID; keep a photo of your passport on your phone.
  • Leave valuables secured aboard or in your hotel safe.
  • Use bank ATMs inside branches or malls, ideally in daylight.
  • Call 911 for police, fire or ambulance. Ask for an English-speaking operator if needed.
  • Private hospitals with 24-hour emergency care include Hospiten (998 881 3700), Galenia (998 891 5200) and AmeriMed (998 881 3400).
  • Private hospitals expect payment or insurance up front — bring ID, your insurance card and any prior authorization.
  • Follow your ship's medical and insurance procedures before seeking non-emergency care ashore.
  • Inform the ship of any incidents as soon as practical. For consular help, contact the Consular Agency of Canada (998 883 3360).

Cancún & Maya History

From Maya coast to planned resort
A Resort Built From Scratch 🏗️
Few big tourist cities are as young as Cancún. In the late 1960s the long sandy island that is now the Hotel Zone was home to just three caretakers of a coconut plantation, and the nearest settlement, Puerto Juárez, had little more than 100 people. Mexico's government picked the site for a planned resort, and within a few decades it became a major international holiday destination — on a coast the Maya had sailed and fished for centuries.

Long before the resorts, the Maya used this coast for fishing and maritime trade. You can still see their settlements in the middle of the Hotel Zone, at El Rey and at San Miguelito beside the Museo Maya de Cancún.

Further afield are the great Maya cities: Chichén Itzá inland in Yucatán, and Tulum on its cliff above the Caribbean.

The name is usually traced to the Maya kàan kun. The most accepted translation is "nest of snakes", while "place of the golden snake" is a less accepted alternative — and some scholars question whether the name is Maya at all.

Before that, the island was known as Nizuc, meaning "promontory" or "point of grass". The name survives today at Punta Nizuc, at the southern end of the Hotel Zone.

In 1969 FONATUR, Mexico's national tourism fund, chose this stretch of coast for a new resort. Construction began on 20 April 1970 — still celebrated as Cancún's founding day — and the city was formally established in August 1971.

The first hotels and the airport opened in 1974, when the project was still a considerable gamble. Today the Hotel Zone stretches along the Boulevard Kukulcán between the Caribbean and the Nichupté Lagoon.

Sitting on a thin strip of sand, the Hotel Zone is exposed to hurricanes. Gilbert struck on 14 September 1988 as a Category 5 storm, and the damage led to large-scale rebuilding and modernization.

Wilma hit on 21 October 2005 and stalled over Cancún, causing widespread destruction. Federal funding of about US$253 million followed, and FONATUR's director later called the rebuilt resort the best Cancún in its history.

The Mexican Caribbean is part of the Mesoamerican Barrier Reef System, considered the second-largest reef system in the world. It stretches more than 1,000 km from the tip of the Yucatán Peninsula past Belize and Guatemala to Honduras.

MUSA, the underwater museum off Cancún and Isla Mujeres, was created to take pressure off the natural reef: its sculptures act as artificial reefs that corals and fish colonize over time.
