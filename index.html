  • Currency: Pound sterling (£). Scottish banknotes (Bank of Scotland, Royal Bank of Scotland, Clydesdale) are legal tender in Scotland and are accepted alongside Bank of England notes, though some shops in England may refuse them. Contactless card and phone payments are accepted almost everywhere.
  • ATMs: Widely available across Leith and the city centre, including at Ocean Terminal. Withdrawals are free from most UK machines.
  • Language: English, with a strong Scots accent and plenty of dialect. Gaelic is only occasionally heard, mostly on signage.
  • Phones: Check your roaming plan before landing. UK country code is +44; Edinburgh landlines start 0131. Power sockets are Type G (three-pin, 230V) — bring an adaptor.
  • Drinking age: 18. Scotland has a minimum unit price on alcohol, so drinks are not especially cheap.
  • Have your hotel name, address, and phone number ready before requesting leave.
  • Provide a contact number where you can be reached at all times.
  • Know your leave expiry time and plan return travel accordingly.
  • If your plans change, inform the ship per routine as soon as possible.
  • Keep your military ID and leave pass on your person.
  • Edinburgh is a big, busy city. Leith is about 3–4 km from the centre — allow time for buses or trams on the way back, particularly at night.

What to Expect
Edinburgh has a mild, temperate maritime climate — cool, changeable, and often breezy. Summers (June–August) are comfortable, with highs around 17–20°C and very long days (light until well after 10 pm in June). Spring and autumn average 7–14°C. Winters are short on daylight and hover around 2–7°C with occasional frost and rare lying snow. Wind off the Firth of Forth makes it feel colder than the thermometer says, and the haar can roll in on spring and summer mornings. Pack a waterproof and layers year-round. The port is in Leith, about 3–4 km from the city centre. The Old Town and New Town are compact and very walkable once you get there, but hilly and cobbled. Buses and trams link Leith to Princes Street in roughly 15–25 minutes.

Transportation
  • Walking: Leith to the Old Town is roughly 45–60 minutes on foot along Leith Walk. Fine in good weather, but hilly at the city end.
  • Bus: Lothian Buses run frequently between Leith and the city centre, day and night. Tap on with a contactless card or use the Lothian app; exact change is needed if paying cash.
  • Tram: The Edinburgh tram runs from the Airport through Princes Street and down Leith Walk to Newhaven. Buy tickets at the stop machines or the app, or tap on with contactless.
  • Taxis & rideshare: Black cabs can be hailed or found at ranks; Uber and Bolt both operate in the city.
  • Train: Waverley Station is in the heart of the city and has services across Scotland and to London. Haymarket handles west and Fife services.
  • Drive on the left: If renting a car, note that the UK drives on the left. Parking in the centre is limited and expensive; public transport is easier.
  • Day trips: South Queensferry (Forth Bridges) is close by train or bus. Stirling, St Andrews, and Glasgow are all within about an hour or so by train.

Food, Coffee & Bars
Seafood, whisky, and a lot of pubs
Edinburgh has an excellent food scene for its size — fresh Scottish seafood, game, and a strong specialty-coffee culture, plus the Michelin-starred restaurants of Leith right by the port. Reservations are recommended for dinner, especially on weekends and during the Festival in August. Try a traditional haggis, neeps & tatties at least once.
Hotels
Edinburgh & Leith · Book early — the city fills up
Edinburgh has hotels at every price point, but demand is high — particularly in August (Festival Fringe and the Military Tattoo), at Hogmanay (New Year), and on rugby weekends. Book as early as possible. Staying in Leith keeps you close to the ship; staying in the Old or New Town puts you in the middle of the sights.
Safety
Stay sharp, stay safe

  • Edinburgh is one of the safer UK cities, but it is a large, busy capital with a lively nightlife. Normal big-city awareness applies.
  • Pickpockets work crowded areas — the Royal Mile, Princes Street, and Waverley Station, especially during the Festival.
  • Stay aware of your surroundings at night, particularly around the Grassmarket, Cowgate, and Rose Street.
  • Traffic drives on the left. Look right first when crossing, and watch for trams and buses.
  • Buddy system, especially at night.
  • The weather turns quickly. Carry a waterproof and a warm layer even on a sunny morning.
  • Cobbles and steps can be very slippery when wet, especially in the closes off the Royal Mile.
  • Arthur's Seat and Salisbury Crags have steep, loose paths and exposed edges. Wear proper footwear and avoid them in low cloud, high wind, or after dark.
  • Haar (sea fog) can reduce visibility along the waterfront and on the hills.
  • Winter daylight is short — sunset is as early as 3:30 pm in December.
  • The pubs are good and plentiful. Pace yourself — pints and whisky measures are generous.
  • Know how you're getting back to the ship before you go out. Night buses run, and taxis and Uber are available, but expect delays at closing time.
  • Public drinking is restricted in Scotland and can result in a fine. Keep it to licensed premises.
  • Look out for your shipmates.
  • You're representing the ship — be a good guest.
  • Carry your military ID and a second piece of government photo ID. Bars may ask for ID if you look under 25.
  • Don't carry more cash than you need — cards and contactless are accepted nearly everywhere.
  • Keep phones and wallets in front pockets or zipped bags in crowds.
  • Leave valuables secured aboard or in your hotel safe.
  • Call 999 (or 112) for police, fire, ambulance, or the Coastguard.
  • Call 101 for non-emergency police matters.
  • Call 111 (NHS 24) for urgent but non-life-threatening medical advice.
  • Hospital: Royal Infirmary of Edinburgh, Little France — the main A&E for the city.
  • Follow your ship's medical and insurance procedures before seeking non-emergency care ashore.
  • Inform the ship of any incidents as soon as practical.

Edinburgh & Leith History
Castle, Enlightenment, and a very old port
A Capital Built on a Volcano
Edinburgh has been Scotland's capital for over 600 years. Its Old Town sits on a ridge of volcanic rock leading down from the Castle, while the Georgian New Town to the north is a planned masterpiece of neoclassical streets and squares. Together they form a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Leith, where your ship is berthed, was Edinburgh's port for centuries and has its own long and colourful maritime story.

Edinburgh Castle stands on Castle Rock, an extinct volcanic plug, and the site has been occupied since the Iron Age. For centuries it was a royal residence and the key fortress in Scotland's wars with England.

The Royal Mile runs down the ridge from the Castle to the Palace of Holyroodhouse. Confined by the city walls, the medieval town grew upward — tenements of ten or more storeys, with narrow closes and wynds running between them. Many of those closes are still walkable today.

The Castle's One O'Clock Gun has been fired Monday to Saturday since 1861, originally so ships in the Forth could set their chronometers.

In the 18th century Edinburgh became one of Europe's great intellectual centres. Philosophers and scientists including David Hume, Adam Smith, and James Hutton worked here, and the period became known as the Scottish Enlightenment.

As the Old Town grew overcrowded, the city built a new, planned district to the north from the 1760s. The New Town's wide streets, squares, and classical buildings are still among the finest examples of Georgian town planning anywhere. The Old and New Towns were inscribed as a World Heritage Site in 1995.

Leith has been Edinburgh's harbour for around 900 years, trading wine, wool, timber, and whisky across the North Sea and beyond. It was once a separate burgh and only merged with Edinburgh in 1920 — some locals still insist it's a separate place.

Leith's long link to France dates to the Auld Alliance. In the 16th century French troops garrisoned the port, and it was besieged in 1560. The harbour later grew into a major whaling, shipbuilding, and bonded-wine-and-whisky centre. Its warehouses and quaysides are still visible around the Shore.

The Royal Yacht Britannia, launched in 1953 and decommissioned in 1997, has been berthed at Ocean Terminal in Leith since 1998.

Scotch whisky is legally defined and must be distilled and aged in Scotland for at least three years. Leith was one of the main places where blended whisky was matured and bottled, and the port shipped it around the world.

Whisky is made in several regions, each with its own style — smoky Islay, honeyed Speyside, light Lowland, and more. Edinburgh is a good place to try a range side by side; the Scotch Whisky Experience and the Bow Bar are two popular options. Whisky is usually served neat or with a splash of water — ask the bartender.

Every August, Edinburgh hosts the world's largest arts festival — the Fringe — alongside the International Festival, the Book Festival, and the Royal Edinburgh Military Tattoo on the Castle Esplanade. The city's population effectively doubles and prices rise accordingly.

Hogmanay (New Year's Eve) is the other big event, with street parties and fireworks. Since 1999 the city has also been home to the Scottish Parliament, which sits at Holyrood.

  • 1128 — Holyrood Abbey founded by King David I.
  • 1560 — Siege of Leith ends French influence in Scotland.
  • 1707 — Act of Union joins Scotland and England; the Scottish Parliament is dissolved.
  • 1767 — Work begins on the planned New Town.
  • 1861 — The One O'Clock Gun is first fired from the Castle.
  • 1920 — Leith merges with Edinburgh.
  • 1947 — The first Edinburgh International Festival and Fringe.
  • 1995 — Old and New Towns become a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
  • 1999 — Scottish Parliament reconvenes; the Holyrood building opens in 2004.
  • Today — A living capital: castles, galleries, festivals, and a port that still sees ships from all over the world.

Edinburgh Castle — The essential stop. Allow at least two hours, and book tickets online if you can.

The Shore, Leith — Walk the old harbour. Several former warehouses are now restaurants and bars.

Royal Yacht Britannia — Tour the royal apartments and the engine room at Ocean Terminal, a short walk from the port.
